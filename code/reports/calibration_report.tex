\maketitle
\vspace{-0.6cm}

\noindent\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Small open economy with overlapping generations, solved in detrended
  per-capita units. A hat denotes $\hat x_t=X_t/(Z_tN_t)$, with
  $Z_t=(1+g)^t$ labour productivity and $N_t$ the population. Stocks
  accumulate at the gross rate $\Gamma_t=(1+g)(1+n_t)$.
\item On the balanced growth path $\hat x$ is constant, levels grow at
  $\Gamma-1$ and per-capita quantities at $g$.
\item Preferences are $\log c-\nu\ell^{1+\varphi}/(1+\varphi)$. The period is
  a year.
\item Households hold domestic capital and foreign assets at the world return
  $r$. Government debt is held by official creditors at the real rate $r_B$.
\item TFP $A$ is set so that $\hat y=1$ in 2023, so ratios to output read as
  levels.
\item Wealth sits on a grid of 100 nodes from 0 to 50 times output per person,
  denser near zero (spacing $u^{1.5}$). Saving choices are grid nodes.
  Aggregates come from the distribution over household states carried forward
  by age, not from simulation.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\footnotesize
Sources for the parameters not taken straight from a published table:
\begin{itemize}
\item $K_g/Y$: the IMF ICSD public capital stock for 2019, carried to 2023 by
  perpetual inventory with national-accounts public investment and real GDP.
\item $\delta_g$: estimated from the same stock over 2000--2019; within 0.001
  of $I_g/K_g-(\Gamma_0-1)$.
\item $r_B$: a real rate, the 2026--60 mean of the real effective interest
  rate in the Commission's debt projection for Greece, which deflates the
  nominal effective rate on the stock by GDP deflator growth (Spring 2026
  forecast, Debt Sustainability Monitor layout). The past gives the same
  number: the implicit nominal rate averaged 1.9\% over 2012--24 and deflator
  growth 0.9\%.
\item $b_{min}$: the national pension, \EUR{413.76} a month from January
  2023, over output per person aged 25--84 (\EUR{224.7}bn of GDP over 7.56
  million people, \EUR{29{,}722} each). The amount is from the legislation
  and the 2024 Ageing Report country fiche; Eurostat reports only aggregate
  pension spending.
\item Retirement age: the average effective age at which people start an
  old-age, early or disability pension, 63.8 in 2022 against a statutory 67.
  To 2070 it follows the Ageing Report's projection (country fiche, Table 4):
  65.5 in 2030, 66.4 in 2040, 66.6 in 2050, 67.4 in 2060 and 67.9 in 2070,
  linear in between. After 2070 it rises one for one with life expectancy at
  65 (men and women averaged, EUROPOP2023), the link of Law 4336/2015, and
  reaches 70.3 in 2100. A cohort retires at the age in force when it gets
  there: 67.7 for the cohort entering in 2023, 69.9 for the one entering in
  2050. The model is annual, so each cohort is split between the two whole
  ages around its average, in the proportions that reproduce it.
\item Pension index: the replacement rate in year $t$ is $\rho^{pens}$ times
  an index equal to one in 2023. The index is the Ageing Report's average
  pension relative to GDP per employed person, pension spending over GDP
  divided by pensioners over employment (country fiche, Tables 6 and 10):
  0.85 in 2030, 0.77 in 2040, 0.67 in 2050 and 0.62 from 2060, linear in
  between. It applies to every pension in payment, $b_{min}$ included.
  Households know the path from entry, as they know their cohort's retirement
  age and survival rates.
\item Unemployment rates by education: 2023, ages 25--64 (Eurostat
  \texttt{lfsa\_urgaed}).
\item Education shares: the population aged 25--84 in 2023, from the labour
  force survey for ages 25--74 (\texttt{lfsa\_pgaed}) and the 2021 census for
  ages 75--84 (\texttt{cens\_21ae\_r2}).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Demographics}
\vspace{-0.1cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
\item The calibration starts from the measured 2023 age distribution, not a
  stationary population. A stationary population with the same dependency
  ratio would miss the large cohorts about to retire, which drive the
  projected rise in the ratio.
\item Each cohort faces the survival rates of its own birth year.
\item From 2023, survival and the size of entering cohorts follow the
  EUROPOP2023 baseline to 2100, in the baseline and in every experiment.
  After 2100 mortality stays at its 2100 level and the growth of entering
  cohorts goes to zero by 2120. The age distribution is constant from 2179 on.
\item Matched exactly: the population aged 25--84, every year; the 2023 age
  distribution, at every age; death rates by year and age.
\item Missed: the age composition after 2023, because net migration at ages
  26--84 is replaced by a change in the cohort entering at 25, so the model
  has too many prime-age and old people until the 2050s and too few after
  (persons aged 65--84 per person aged 25--64: 0.69 in 2050 and 0.48 in 2080,
  against 0.66 and 0.52 in the projection); people under 25 and over 84 (the
  85+ group is 4\% of the population in 2023 and 10\% in 2100); what has
  happened since 2022 (recorded births in 2023--24 are 11--14\% below the
  projection's).
\item Net migration at every age is put into the cohort entering at 25
  (Figure~\ref{fig:demography}, top left). It is negative until the 2050s,
  hence the fall in 2024 and the movements through the 2030s.
\item The drop in 2048 is EUROPOP2023's own: its 2022 birth cohort is 9\%
  smaller than 2021's, matching the recorded fall in live births from 85.3 to
  75.9 thousand (Eurostat \texttt{demo\_fasec}). The projection starts from
  the population of 1 January 2022, so later cohorts are projected: 79.2 and
  77.8 thousand births for 2023 and 2024 against 71.2 and 68.3 recorded, so
  the cohorts entering from 2049 on are 10--14\% larger than recorded births
  imply.
\item The dip in 2084--90 is the exit at 85 of the small 2024--29 cohorts.
\item The share aged 65--84 rises from 0.26 to 0.41 by 2050 as the cohorts
  born in the 1960s and 1970s retire. Persons aged 65--84 per person aged
  25--64 peak at 0.69 in 2051, from 0.36 in 2023. The retired per non-retired
  person peak at 0.60 and fall to 0.29 by 2100, below today's value, because
  the retirement age rises and people above 84 are outside the model.
\item Because the 2023 population is not stationary, the economy is in
  transition even with no policy change. Prices do not move: the return is
  set abroad and the wage follows from it. Ageing reaches households through
  the retirement age, higher for later cohorts, and through the pension index,
  which lowers the pension a given career earns.
\item Output per person falls to 2051 (Figure~\ref{fig:aggregates}) and
  pensions rise to 2050 (Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal}) as the share aged 40--64
  shrinks and the retired per non-retired person grow. Both turn around once
  the smaller cohorts reach old age and the retirement age keeps rising.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Baseline transition}\vspace{-0.2cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
Aggregates (Figure~\ref{fig:aggregates}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Domestic capital and hours move with output: the world return fixes
  $K^{dom}/L$ and public capital per person is constant.
\item Private investment is $I_t=\Gamma_t K^{dom}_{t+1}-(1-\delta)K^{dom}_t$.
  It is plotted as a 5-year centred moving average: as the change in a stock
  some 15 times larger, it magnifies the stock's year-to-year movement about
  20-fold.
\item Household wealth is $A=K^{dom}+NFA$.
\item All movement comes from the age structure and the rising retirement
  age.
\item Output per person bottoms out at 0.87 of its 2023 level in 2051 and
  recovers to 1.06 by 2100 on the rising retirement age.
\item Real growth averages 0.65\% a year over 2026--60 and 0.20\% in
  2041--50, when the retirement age rises only 0.2 years over the decade
  while the working-age population shrinks.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Government budget (Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal}, Table~\ref{tab:projection}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Tax rates, $G/Y$ and defence$/Y$ are fixed. Pensions follow the index
  and the number of retirees. Health spending and the tax bases follow the
  age structure.
\item Other net spending $O$ stands for the government spending and revenue
  the model does not describe: everything in the primary balance other than
  pensions, unemployment benefits, the minimum income, public health,
  government consumption, defence, public investment and the four taxes. No
  household pays or receives it. It is negative because the revenue it stands
  in for exceeds the spending.
\item $O$ is set so that the primary balance equals its 2023 value in the
  data (1.95\% of output), the Commission's projection over 2024--60, and
  from 2061 the balance that holds the debt ratio at its 2060 value.
\item Debt is the end-of-year stock over the year's output,
  $d_t=d_{t-1}(1+r_B)/(1+g_t)-pb_t+sfa_t$ from 1.64 in 2023, with
  $g_t=\Gamma_{t-1}\hat y_t/\hat y_{t-1}-1$.
\item The stock-flow adjustment $sfa$ is the change in debt not explained by
  the deficit. It is set to reproduce the debt ratios of 2024 (154.2\%) and
  2025 (146.1\%). Over 2026--60 it is the projection's (0.8--1.1\% of output
  a year to 2032, interest on the official loans whose payment is deferred).
  After 2060 it is zero.
\item Debt falls to 136.9\% in 2030 and 129.1\% in 2040, rises to 134.6\% in
  2050 and settles at 125.8\% from 2060. The projection reaches 103.4\% in
  2060 (Figure~\ref{fig:projection}).
\end{itemize}

\noindent Why the debt paths differ:
\begin{itemize}
\item The rate. $r_B$ is the 2026--60 mean of the projection's real effective
  rate. The projection's rate is a path: $-0.65$\% on average over 2026--32,
  when the nominal rate on the official loans is below deflator growth, and
  1.47\% over 2033--60. With debt at 140\% of output, the early difference
  adds about 2.4 points a year to the model's ratio. $r_B$ does not enter
  the household problem, so the debt recursion could use the projection's
  path instead; the model's ratio would then be 115.5\% in 2040, 127.3\% in
  2050 and 122.6\% in 2060.
\item Growth. $g$ is productivity growth per worker, not output growth.
  Output growth is $(1+g)(1+n_t)\hat y_{t+1}/\hat y_t-1$: over 2026--60,
  1.70\% ($g$) $-0.75$\% (population aged 25--84) $-0.29$\% (hours per
  person) $=0.65$\%, against 1.08\% in the projection; in 2041--50, 1.70
  $-0.83-0.65=0.20$\% against 1.17\%. The productivity assumption is the
  Ageing Report's in both. The difference is employment: the Report has the
  employment rate of 20--64-year-olds rising from 66\% in 2022 to 74\% in
  2050 and 75\% in 2070, so employment falls much less than the working-age
  population. About half of the rise is unemployment, from 12.4\% of the
  labour force in 2022 to 6.5\% from 2060; the other half is the
  participation of 55--74-year-olds, which the rising retirement age
  delivers (country fiche, Table 3). In the model the retirement age follows
  the fiche, participation and unemployment rates stay at their 2023
  values, and hours per person fall with the age structure.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Spending, revenue and the balance (Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal}, Table~\ref{tab:projection}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Pensions fall from 16.0\% of output in 2023 to 14.2\% in 2030, because
  the index falls faster than the number of retirees grows. They climb back
  to 15.9\% in 2050 as the large cohorts retire, then fall to 13.2\% in 2060
  and 10.3\% in 2070 once the index settles and the retirement age keeps
  rising.
\item The Ageing Report has 14.5\% of GDP in 2022, 12.7\% in 2030, 14.0\% in
  2050 and 12.0\% in 2070, on the Ageing Working Group definition. The
  model's level is the national-accounts 16\% of 2023.
\item Public health spending rises from 5.4\% to 7.5\% of output by 2050,
  with the unit cost by age growing at the trend.
\item Revenue rises from 35.0\% to 38.5\% of output by 2050, mostly
  consumption tax: households save more for their lower pensions, wealth
  rises from 4.0 to 5.3 times output, and the consumption it finances is
  taxed.
\item The primary balance before $O$ is $-6.9$\% of output in 2023, $-4.9$\%
  in 2030 and $-5.6$\% in 2050. So $O/Y$ goes from $-8.9$\% in 2023
  ($-11.2$\% in 2025) to $-7.2$\% in 2030, $-6.2$\% in 2040 and $-5.4$\% in
  2060, then up to $+0.5$\% by 2100 as pensions keep falling. A primary
  deficit of about 0.8\% of output holds debt at 126\%.
\item The projection's growth (Figure~\ref{fig:projection}) is the forecast
  to 2027 and potential growth after. The model has no cycle.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Policy experiments}\vspace{-0.2cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
Design:
\begin{itemize}
\item Two permanent spending increases start in 2023, each worth 2\% of
  output. Neither enters utility.
\item Government consumption $G$ rises by 2\% of each year's output.
\item Public investment $I_g$ rises by 2\% of 2023 output and stays there in
  detrended per-capita terms, because public capital enters production and
  an increase set as a share of output would depend on its own effect.
\item Debt financing: the primary deficit is borrowed abroad at $r_B$ and no
  tax changes.
\item Labour-tax financing: $\tau_l$ rises by a constant $\Delta\tau_l$ from
  2023, set so that in 2202, the last year of the horizon, either the debt
  ratio or net foreign assets over output are back at their baseline values.
\item Other net spending stays at its baseline share of output; the
  stock-flow adjustment stays at its baseline level.
\item The policy is announced in 2023. The wealth each cohort holds that year
  is the same as in the baseline.
\item Net foreign assets are household foreign assets less government debt.
\item The cumulative multiplier is the sum of the output response over the
  sum of the spending increase, both as aggregate flows over the horizon.
\item In Table~\ref{tab:fiscal}, debt is at the end of the year over the
  year's output; the baseline has 126\% from 2060 on and net foreign assets
  of $-0.37$ of output in 2200. The 2023--32 column is the mean deviation
  over those years.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Government consumption (Figure~\ref{fig:G}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Debt-financed, it leaves output, consumption and hours exactly where
  they were. It enters neither utility nor production, and the debt is held
  abroad, so no household budget changes. The multiplier is zero.
\item The debt ratio climbs to 207\% of output in 2060 and 339\% in 2200.
\item Bringing the ratio back to 126\% by 2202 takes $\Delta\tau_l=4.18$
  percentage points. Hours and output fall 0.8\% in 2023 and 0.4\% on average
  over 2023--32, and are back at the baseline by 2050. Consumption is 3.2\%
  below the baseline in 2023 and 4.8\% below in 2100; wealth is 4.5\% below
  in 2100.
\item Returning net foreign assets to their baseline ratio instead takes 4.67
  points and leaves the debt ratio at 102\% in 2200.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Public investment (Figure~\ref{fig:Ig}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Public capital per person rises from 0.70 of 2023 output to 0.99 in
  2050 and 1.05 in 2100.
\item Debt-financed, output is 1.3\% below the baseline in 2023, because
  households expect higher wages and work less now. It is above the baseline
  from 2026, 2.7\% in 2050 and 3.0\% in 2100. Consumption is 1.2\% above the
  baseline in 2023 and 3.1\% above in 2100.
\item The cumulative multiplier is 0.20 over 2023--32 and 1.49 over the
  horizon.
\item The debt ratio reaches 203\% in 2060 and 299\% in 2200.
\item The labour-tax rules need 3.42 points (debt target) and 3.83 points
  (net-foreign-asset target). Output is 1.6\% below the baseline in 2023
  under both and 2.8--2.9\% above in 2050. Consumption ends 0.9\% and 1.4\%
  below in 2100.
\end{itemize}
