\maketitle
\vspace{-0.6cm}

\noindent\small
The model is a small open economy with overlapping generations, solved in
detrended per-capita units. A hat denotes $\hat x_t=X_t/(Z_tN_t)$, where
$Z_t=(1+g)^t$ is labour productivity and $N_t$ population, so stocks
accumulate at the gross rate $\Gamma_t=(1+g)(1+n_t)$. On the balanced growth
path $\hat x$ is constant, levels grow at $\Gamma-1$ and per-capita
quantities at $g$. Preferences are $\log c-\nu\ell^{1+\varphi}/(1+\varphi)$.
Households hold domestic capital and foreign assets, both earning $r$.
Government debt is held abroad by official creditors at the real rate $r_B$.
TFP $A$ is set so that $\hat y=1$ in 2023, so ratios to output can be read as
levels. The period is a year.

\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\footnotesize
Sources for the externally set parameters that are not read directly from a
table: $K_g/Y$ is the IMF ICSD public capital stock for 2019, carried forward
to 2023 by perpetual inventory with national-accounts public investment and
real GDP. $\delta_g$ is estimated from the ICSD stock over 2000--2019; it is
within 0.001 of $I_g/K_g-(\Gamma_0-1)$. $r_B$ is a real rate: the mean over
2026--60 of the real effective interest rate in the Commission's debt
projection for Greece, which is the nominal effective rate on the stock
deflated by GDP deflator growth (Spring 2026 Economic Forecast, in the layout
of the Debt Sustainability Monitor). The nominal implicit rate on the stock
averaged 1.9\% over 2012--24 and deflator growth 0.9\%, so the same mean
over the past is also 1.05\%. $b_{min}$ is the Greek national pension,
\EUR{413.76} a month from January 2023, divided by output per person aged
25--84: \EUR{224.7}bn of GDP (Eurostat) over 7{,}559{,}564 people, or
\EUR{29{,}722} each. The pension amount comes from the legislation and the
2024 Ageing Report country fiche, since Eurostat reports only aggregate pension
spending.

The retirement age is the average effective retirement age, the age at which
people start an old-age, early or disability pension: 63.8 in 2022, against a
statutory 67. Its path to 2070 is the Ageing Report's projection (country
fiche, Table 4): 65.5 in 2030, 66.4 in 2040, 66.6 in 2050, 67.4 in 2060 and
67.9 in 2070, linear between those years. After 2070 it moves one for one with
life expectancy at 65 at the three-yearly reviews of Law 4336/2015, with life
expectancy the mean of men and women in the EUROPOP2023 baseline projection;
it reaches 70.4 in 2100 and stays there. A cohort retires on average at the
age in force when it reaches it, 67.7 for cohorts entering in 2023 and 69.9
for those entering in 2050. Since the model is annual, each cohort is split
between the two whole ages around that average, in the proportions that
reproduce it.

The replacement rate of year $t$ is $\rho^{pens}$ times an index, equal to
one in 2023. The index is the Ageing Report's projection of the average pension
relative to GDP per employed person: public pension spending over GDP divided
by pensioners over employment (country fiche, Tables 6 and 10), 0.85 in 2030,
0.77 in 2040, 0.67 in 2050, 0.62 in 2060 and 0.62 from 2070 on, linear
between those years. It applies to every pension in payment in the year, and
$b_{min}$ follows it. Households know the path from entry, as they know the
retirement age and survival rates of their cohort. The unemployment rates by
education are the 2023 rates for ages 25--64, the model's working ages
(Eurostat \texttt{lfsa\_urgaed}). The education shares are those of the
population aged 25--84 in 2023: the labour force survey for ages 25--74
(Eurostat \texttt{lfsa\_pgaed}) and, for ages 75--84, which the survey tables
do not cover, the 2021 census by cohort (Eurostat \texttt{cens\_21ae\_r2}).

\section*{Demographics}
\vspace{-0.1cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
The calibration uses the measured 2023 age distribution, not a stationary
population.\footnote{A stationary population with the same dependency ratio
would miss the age profile, in particular the large cohorts about to retire
that drive the projected rise in the ratio.} Each cohort faces the survival
rates of its own birth year. After 2023, survival and the size of entering
cohorts follow the EUROPOP2023 baseline projection to 2100, in the baseline and
in every policy experiment. After 2100, mortality stays at its 2100 level and
the growth of entering cohorts falls to zero by 2120. The age distribution is
constant from 2179 on.

Since the 2023 population is not stationary, the economy is in transition
even without any policy change. Prices do not move: the return is set abroad
and the wage follows from it. Ageing changes household decisions through the
retirement age, which is higher for later cohorts, and through the pension
index, which lowers the pension a given career earns. Households also respond
once a tax adjusts to finance the budget, as in the tax-financed experiments.

\noindent\footnotesize
Pensions fall from 16.0\% of output in 2023 to 14.3\% in 2030, as the pension
index falls faster than the number of retirees rises, then rise to 15.8\% in
2050 as the large cohorts retire, and fall to 13.1\% in 2060 and 10.2\% in 2070
as the index settles and the retirement age keeps rising. The Ageing Report's
projection has 14.5\% of GDP in 2022, 12.7\% in 2030, 14.0\% in 2050 and 12.0\%
in 2070. Public health spending rises from 5.4\% of output to 7.4\% in 2050,
with the unit cost by age growing at the trend. Revenue rises from 34.9\% of
output to 38.8\% in 2050, mostly through the consumption tax: households save
more for the lower pensions, wealth rises from 4.0 to 5.5 times output by 2050,
and the consumption it finances is taxed. The primary balance before other net
spending is $-7.1$\% of output in 2023, $-4.8$\% in 2030 and $-5.3$\% in 2050,
so $O/Y$ is $-9.1$\% in 2023, $-11.2$\% in 2025, $-7.1$\% in 2030, $-6.1$\% in
2040 and $-5.0$\% in 2060. After 2060 it rises to $+0.7$\% by 2100 as pensions
keep falling, with a primary deficit of about 0.8\% of output holding debt at
125\%. Output per person falls to 0.87 of its 2023 level in 2051 and recovers
to 1.06 by 2100 as the retirement age rises. Real output growth averages
0.66\% a year over 2026--60, against 1.08\% in the projection; in 2041--50 it
is 0.24\% against 1.17\%, when the retirement age in the Ageing Report's path
rises by 0.2 years in the decade while the population of working age falls.
