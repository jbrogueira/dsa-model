\noindent\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Small open economy with overlapping generations, solved in detrended
  per-capita units. A hat denotes $\hat x_t=X_t/(Z_tN_t)$, with
  $Z_t=(1+g)^t$ labour productivity and $N_t$ the population. Stocks
  accumulate at the gross rate $\Gamma_t=(1+g)(1+n_t)$.
\item On the balanced growth path $\hat x$ is constant, levels grow at
  $\Gamma-1$ and per-capita quantities at $g$.
\item Preferences are $\log c-\nu\ell^{1+\varphi}/(1+\varphi)$. The period is
  a year.
\item Households hold domestic capital and foreign assets at the world return
  $r$. Government debt is held by official creditors at the real rate
  $r_{B,t}$: the data's real effective rate to 2024, a proxy for 2025, over
  2026--60 the rate of the Debt Sustainability Monitor forecast prepared at
  the European Stability Mechanism on the Commission's Spring 2026 forecast,
  hereafter the DSM forecast (ESM), and 2\% from 2070.
\item Firms pay a tax $\tau_y$ on gross output, so that
  $(1-\tau_y)\alpha Y/K=r+\delta$ and $w=(1-\tau_y)(1-\alpha)Y/L$. It stands
  for the taxes on production and the other revenue the model has no line
  for, net of the spending it has no line for. The rate is set in 2023 to the
  outturn primary balance and held through 2025; from 2026 it takes the
  constant value at which the debt ratio of 2060 equals the DSM forecast
  (ESM), and keeps it.
\item Every adult receives a lump-sum transfer, the same at every age and
  education level and untaxed, worth $\lambda$ of output per person, output
  taken as a centred five-year average: the cash and in-kind benefits
  outside pensions, unemployment and health. Households choose next
  period's wealth on the asset grid, so a transfer tied to each year's own
  output would let their choices and the transfer sustain a two-year
  alternation in hours.
\item The government buys education, with the number of pupils from
  EUROPOP2023 and the cost per pupil moving with the wage, and receives the
  net transfer from the EU budget.
\item TFP $A$ is set so that $\hat y=1$ in 2023, so ratios to output read as
  levels.
\item Wealth sits on a grid of 100 nodes from 0 to 50 times output per person,
  denser near zero (spacing $u^{1.5}$). Saving choices are grid nodes.
  Aggregates come from the distribution over household states carried forward
  by age, not from simulation.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\footnotesize
Sources for the parameters not taken straight from a published table:
\begin{itemize}
\item $K_g/Y$: the IMF ICSD public capital stock for 2019, carried to 2023 by
  perpetual inventory with national-accounts public investment and real GDP
  (Figure~\ref{fig:public_capital}).
\item $\delta_g$: $I_g/K_g-(\Gamma_0-1)$ at the 2023 investment share, which
  keeps public capital per person constant, with the growth factor of the
  demography in use when it was set ($\Gamma_0-1=1.21\%$); at the current
  1.37\% the same rule makes investment 4.1\% of output rather than 4.0.
  The perpetual-inventory estimate from the same stock over 2000--2019 is
  0.003 lower.
\item $r_B$: a real rate by year. For 2023 and 2024 the data's real effective
  rate, interest payments over the previous year's debt deflated by GDP
  deflator growth ($-4.1$ and $-1.1$\%); for 2025 the projection's 2026
  nominal rate deflated by 2025 deflator growth ($-1.0$\%); over 2026--60
  the real effective rate of the DSM forecast (ESM), a projection for
  Greece in the layout of the Commission's Debt Sustainability Monitor on
  the Spring 2026 forecast, prepared at the European Stability Mechanism
  (file of 28 September 2026): $-1.1$\% in
  2026, $-0.6$\% in 2030, 1.7\% in 2040, 1.6\% in 2050 and 1.2\% in 2060;
  linear to 2\% over 2061--70, the Monitor's long-run real rate on market
  debt; 2\% after.
\item $\tau_y$: the rate that gives the outturn primary balance of 2023
  (1.95\% of GDP) at the calibrated household block, solved together with
  $A$, and held through 2025. From 2026 one constant rate, solved with the
  transition so that the debt ratio of 2060 equals the DSM forecast's
  (103.4\%), and held for the rest of the horizon. The scenario
  with the 2023 rate held throughout is the model's own reading of the
  accounts (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}).
\item $G/Y$, $D/Y$, $I_g/Y$, $e_0$, $\lambda$ and the pension and health
  targets: general government accounts 2023 (Eurostat \texttt{gov\_10a\_main},
  \texttt{gov\_10a\_exp}), purchases net of the imputed contributions of
  civil servants, the imputed contributions allocated across functions in
  proportion to the wage bill. Own wage bill and intermediate consumption
  outside defence, health and education 7.0\% of GDP; defence 1.66\%; gross
  fixed capital formation 4.0\%; education 2.6\%; cash benefits other than
  pensions and unemployment benefits plus transfers in kind outside health
  3.5\%; pensions 14.0\%, old age, survivors and disability, the Ageing
  Report's concept (ESSPROS, \texttt{spr\_exp\_pens}, 2023; the accounts'
  old-age and survivors functions alone give the same 14.0); public health
  5.3\%, of a total of 8.4\% with household health consumption from the
  national accounts; actual social contributions 11.2\%.
\item Health cost by age: the Ageing Report's per-capita public health
  spending by five-year age group for the EU14 (2024 Ageing Report,
  projections volume, Institutional Paper 279, Graph I.2.2, p.~68), each
  group at its midpoint from 27 to 97, linear between midpoints,
  flat outside, normalised to a population-weighted mean of one over the 2023
  cross-section. The level is $m$, set in the SMM.
\item Net transfer from the EU budget: current and capital transfers
  received from EU institutions less own resources paid, 1.9\% of GDP in
  2023, 1.8\% in 2024, 2.7\% in 2025 and 2026 (Recovery and Resilience
  Facility grants, own resources held at their 2023 share of GDP), and
  1.0\% from 2027, an assumption for the next financial framework: the
  government's net receipts were 0.8\% in 2019 and 1.5--1.6\% in 2020--22.
\item $b_{min}$: the national pension, \EUR{413.76} a month from January
  2023, over output per person aged 25--99 (\EUR{224.7}bn of GDP over 7.92
  million people, \EUR{28{,}353} each). The amount is from the legislation
  and the 2024 Ageing Report country fiche; Eurostat reports only aggregate
  pension spending.
\item Retirement age: the average effective age at which people start an
  old-age, early or disability pension, 63.8 in 2022 against a statutory 67.
  To 2070 it follows the Ageing Report's projection (country fiche, Table 4):
  65.5 in 2030, 66.4 in 2040, 66.6 in 2050, 67.4 in 2060 and 67.9 in 2070,
  linear in between. After 2070 it rises by 0.75 of the change in life
  expectancy at 65 (men and women averaged, EUROPOP2023), the ratio of the
  fiche's rise in the effective age (4.1 years) to its rise in the statutory
  age (5.5 years) over 2022--70, and reaches 69.7 in 2100. A cohort retires
  at the age in force when it gets there: 67.7 for the cohort entering in
  2023, 69.4 for the one entering in 2050. The model is annual, so each cohort is split between the two whole
  ages around its average, in the proportions that reproduce it.
\item Pension index: the replacement rate in year $t$ is $\rho^{pens}$ times
  an index equal to one in 2023. The index is the Ageing Report's average
  pension relative to GDP per employed person, pension spending over GDP
  divided by pensioners over employment (country fiche, Tables 6 and 10):
  0.85 in 2030, 0.77 in 2040, 0.67 in 2050 and 0.62 in 2060 and 2070,
  linear in between and constant after 2070. It applies to every pension in payment, $b_{min}$ included.
  Households know the path from entry, as they know their cohort's retirement
  age and survival rates.
\item Unemployment rates by education: 2023, ages 25--64 (Eurostat
  \texttt{lfsa\_urgaed}). Each rate follows an index by calendar year: the
  2024 and 2025 outturns for ages 25--64 (9.5 and 8.3\% against 10.2\% in
  2023), the Spring 2026 forecast for 2026--27 (8.3 and 7.9\% for ages
  15--74, scaled by the 2023 ratio of the two age groups), a linear path to
  the 2024 Ageing Report's 2050 rate (6.6\% for ages 20--64, Institutional
  Paper 279, Table II.1.50, scaled to 5.6\%) and 5.5\% from 2055. The separation rate of each year gives every
  cohort its own income process by age, known from entry.
\item Education shares: the population aged 25--99 in 2023, from the labour
  force survey for ages 25--74 (\texttt{lfsa\_pgaed}) and the 2021 census for
  ages 75--99 (\texttt{cens\_21ae\_r2}).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Demographics}
\vspace{-0.1cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
\item The calibration starts from the 2023 age distribution, smoothed as
  described below, not a stationary population. A stationary population with the same dependency
  ratio would miss the large cohorts about to retire, which drive the
  projected rise in the ratio.
\item Each cohort faces the survival rates of its own birth year: the
  historical life table to 2023 (Eurostat \texttt{demo\_mlifetable}) and the
  EUROPOP2023 mortality assumption from 2024. The historical table ends in an
  open group at 85, so ages 85--99 before 2024 carry the 2022 assumption;
  those entries concern ages a cohort has already passed and enter no
  decision.
\item From 2023, survival and the size of entering cohorts follow the
  EUROPOP2023 baseline to 2100, in the baseline and in every experiment.
  After 2100 mortality stays at its 2100 level and the growth of entering
  cohorts goes to zero by 2120. The age distribution is constant from 2194 on.
\item The size of each entering cohort, measured for the cohorts alive in
  2023 and projected to 2100, is smoothed with a centred five-year moving
  average in logs. Adjacent single-year cohorts in the Greek age pyramid
  differ by 4\% on average and by up to 24\% at the oldest ages, and every
  budget line priced by cohort would otherwise jump with whichever cohort
  enters, retires or dies in a given year.
\item Matched: death rates by year and age, exactly; the population aged
  25--99, within 0.3\% in every year to 2100; the 2023 age distribution, up
  to the smoothing.
\item Missed: the age composition after 2023, because net migration at ages
  26--99 is replaced by a change in the cohort entering at 25, so the model
  has too many prime-age people until the 2040s and too many old people
  until the late 2070s, and too few after (persons aged 65--99 per person
  aged 25--64: 0.88 in 2050 and 0.70 in 2080, against 0.82 and 0.72 in the
  projection); people under 25 and over 99 (the 100+ group is 0.04\% of the
  population in 2023 and 0.5\% in 2100); what has happened since 2022
  (recorded births in 2023--24 are 10--12\% below the projection's).
\item Tables~\ref{tab:demography_short} and \ref{tab:demography} set the
  projection against the data since 2022. On 1 January 2025 the population was 10.37 million, 0.5\% above
  the projection, because net migration was 30 thousand in 2023 and 54
  thousand in 2024 against the projection's 5 and $-2$; live births in
  2023--24 were 10--12\% below the projection's; life expectancy at 65 in
  2024 was 18.7 years for men and 21.8 for women, 0.5 and 0.3 below the
  assumption. The old-age ratio on 1 January 2025, 40.9 per 100, is the
  projection's.
\item Net migration at every age is put into the cohort entering at 25
  (Figure~\ref{fig:demography}, top left). The projection's net migration
  is negative over 2024--29 and small until 2040, so the model's entering
  cohort stays below the projection's 25-year-olds until the 2050s (87
  against 106 thousand in 2030, 81 against 87 in 2050) and above them after
  (90 against 82 in 2070).
\item The projection's cohort of 25-year-olds falls 8\% in 2048, because
  its 2022 birth cohort is 9\% smaller than 2021's, matching the recorded
  fall in live births from 85.3 to 75.9 thousand (Eurostat
  \texttt{demo\_gind}); the smoothing spreads the fall over five years of
  the model's entering cohort. The projection starts from
  the population of 1 January 2022, so later cohorts are projected: 79.2 and
  77.8 thousand births for 2023 and 2024 against 71.2 and 68.3 recorded, so
  the cohorts entering from 2049 on are 10--14\% larger than recorded births
  imply.
\item The share aged 65--99 rises from 0.30 to 0.47 by 2050 as the cohorts
  born in the 1960s and 1970s retire. Persons aged 65--99 per person aged
  25--64 peak at 0.91 in 2056, from 0.43 in 2023. The retired per non-retired
  person rise from 0.46 to 0.80 in 2055 and fall back to 0.49 by 2100,
  because the retirement age rises.
\item Because the 2023 population is not stationary, the economy is in
  transition even with no policy change. The return is set abroad and the
  wage follows from it and from the output tax, so prices move once, with
  the tax in 2026. Ageing reaches households through
  the retirement age, higher for later cohorts, and through the pension index,
  which lowers the pension a given career earns.
\item Output per person falls to 2055 (Figure~\ref{fig:aggregates}) and
  pensions rise to 2051 (Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal}) as the share aged 40--64
  shrinks and the retired per non-retired person grow. Both turn around once
  the smaller cohorts reach old age and the retirement age keeps rising.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Baseline transition}\vspace{-0.2cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
Aggregates (Figure~\ref{fig:aggregates}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Domestic capital and hours move with output while the output tax is
  constant: the world return fixes $K^{dom}/L$ and public capital per person
  is constant. The tax's fall in 2026 raises the wage by 2.5\% and the
  capital-output ratio by 1.7\%; both are constant after.
\item Private investment is $I_t=\Gamma_t K^{dom}_{t+1}-(1-\delta)K^{dom}_t$,
  plotted as a 5-year centred moving average.
\item Household wealth is $A=K^{dom}+NFA+B$; the figure's $NFA$ is household
  foreign assets, $A-K^{dom}$.
\item Output per person is 1.1\% below its 2023 level in 2025, as
  households work less ahead of the higher wage of 2026, rises 6.1\%
  between 2025 and 2030 on that wage and on unemployment falling from 8.3 to
  7.0\%, falls with the age structure to 0.87 of its 2023 level in 2055, and
  recovers to 0.94 by 2070 and 1.05 by 2100 on the rising retirement age.
\item Real growth averages 0.82\% a year over 2026--60: 2.5\% in 2026--30,
  0.5\% in 2031--40, 0.2\% in 2041--50 and 0.9\% in 2051--60, against 1.0,
  0.9, 1.2 and 1.2\% in the DSM forecast (ESM). After the 2026 step
  the yearly path moves by up to a third of a point around its trend,
  because households choose next period's wealth on a grid; the figure
  shows a 5-year centred mean.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Government budget (Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal}, Table~\ref{tab:projection}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Tax rates, $G/Y$ and defence$/Y$ are fixed, and so is public capital
  per person, with investment the flow that keeps it so,
  $(\delta_g+\Gamma_t-1)K_g$, 4.2\% of output in 2023 and rising 5\% with
  the growth factor over the path. Pensions
  follow the index and the number of retirees. Health spending and the tax
  bases follow the age structure; unemployment benefits fall with the
  unemployment rate. Education follows the school-age population and the
  wage. The lump-sum transfer is $\lambda$ of output per person, output
  taken as a centred five-year average.
\item The output tax carries the revenue and the spending the model does
  not describe: 5.95\% of output through 2025, the 2023 pin, and 4.35\%
  from 2026 on, the rate at which the debt ratio of 2060 equals the
  DSM forecast's 103.4\%. The balance over 2026--60 is therefore the one
  the forecast's debt path implies given the model's growth, revenue and
  spending; the model's own balance at the 2023 rate is the scenario of
  Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}.
\item Debt is the end-of-year stock over the year's output,
  $d_t=d_{t-1}(1+r_{B,t})/(1+g_t)-pb_t+sfa_t$ from 1.64 in 2023, with
  $g_t=\Gamma_{t-1}\hat y_t/\hat y_{t-1}-1$. The years 2024 and 2025 are
  history: their ratios are the data's (154.2\%) and the projection's
  starting value (146.1\%), and the flow that reconciles each with the
  recursion is recorded as that year's stock-flow adjustment, $-5.2$ and
  $-2.2$\% of output. The model has neither the real growth of those years
  (2.1\% a year) nor their real rates ($-1.1$ and about $-1.0$\%). Over
  2026--60 the adjustment is the projection's (2.8\% of output in 2026 and
  0.9--1.1\% a year over 2027--32, interest on the official loans whose
  payment is deferred). After 2060 it is zero.
\item The primary balance is 1.9\% of output in 2023, 3.2\% in 2025 and
  between 1.1 and 2.8\% over 2026--60, 1.6\% on average against the
  forecast's 1.4\%: with output growing 0.82\% a year instead of 1.08\%,
  the same debt path needs a balance a quarter of a point higher. Debt is
  121\% of output in 2030, 109\% in 2040, 111\% in 2050 and 103\% in 2060;
  the projection has 125, 111, 111 and 103\%.
\item After 2060 the rate stays at 4.35\% and the balance rises, to 2.5\%
  of output in 2070, 4.0\% in 2080 and 5.1\% in 2100, as pensions fall
  with the rising retirement age while revenue holds its share. Debt falls
  to 86\% in 2070 and 53\% in 2080 and is below zero from 2093: the rate
  that fits the projection to 2060 is far above the one that would hold the
  ratio afterwards.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}, Figure~\ref{fig:projection}):
\begin{itemize}
\item The baseline's rate from 2026 is the one at which the 2060 ratio equals
  the forecast's. With the DSM forecast's primary balance in place of the
  baseline's over 2026--60, everything else as in the model, the ratio in
  2060 would be 113\%; with the forecast's growth in place of the model's,
  94\%. The balance the projection's debt path requires of the model runs a
  quarter of a point of output a year above the forecast's, because the
  model grows 0.82\% a year against the projection's 1.08.
\item With the 2023 rate held throughout, the balance is the model's own:
  3.5\% of output in 2030, 3.3\% in 2040, 2.5\% in 2050 and 2.8\% in 2060,
  1.2 to 2.2 points above the forecast's, and debt falls to 93\% in 2040,
  78\% in 2050, 57\% in 2060 and 26\% in 2070, below zero from 2077.
  Replacing that balance by the forecast's over 2026--60 puts the 2060
  ratio at 119\%; replacing the model's growth, 0.71\% a year, by the
  projection's puts it at 46\%. The gap to the projection is the balance.
  The forecast, following the Commission's method, holds the structural
  balance before the cost of ageing
  at 2.3\% of GDP with a constant revenue ratio and adds the Ageing
  Report's spending paths; the model's revenue rises from 40.8 to 44.8\%
  of output by 2050 with the age structure (consumption tax +1.6 points,
  capital income tax +1.5, bequest tax +1.7), more than its spending.
\item With the forecast's balance, growth and rate the recursion
  reproduces its 103.4\% in 2060.
\item Unemployment. Holding the rate at its 2023 level, 10.2\%, in every
  year, with the same parameters and the same rule, the output tax that
  fits the 2060 projection is 4.94\% of output instead of 4.35: benefits
  are 0.2--0.3 points of output higher, output is 3\% lower and growth over
  2026--60 is 0.69\% a year instead of 0.82, so the same debt path needs
  0.6 point of output more revenue a year.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Spending, revenue and the balance (Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal}, Table~\ref{tab:projection}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Pensions fall from 14.3\% of output in 2023 to 12.1\% in 2030, because
  the index falls faster than the number of retirees grows, climb to 15.1\%
  in 2050 as the large cohorts retire, and fall to 14.0\% in 2060 and 12.2\%
  in 2070. The Ageing Report has 14.5\% of GDP in 2022, 12.7\% in 2030,
  14.0\% in 2050, 12.7\% in 2060 and 12.0\% in 2070. The model is 1.1 points
  above the Report in 2050 and 1.3 above in 2060, and 0.2 above in 2070.
\item Public health spending rises from 5.4\% of output to 7.7\% by 2050
  and 8.0\% by 2060, with the unit cost by age growing at the trend; the
  Ageing Report has +0.4 points by 2040 and +0.6 by 2050, with unit costs
  growing with GDP per capita.
\item Education moves between 2.4 and 2.7\% of output with the school-age
  population; unemployment benefits fall from 0.6 to 0.3\%.
\item Revenue is 41.1\% of output in 2023: consumption tax 9.2, labour
  income tax 6.7, contributions 11.2, capital income tax 3.6, bequest tax
  2.7, output tax 6.0, EU transfer 1.9. Spending is 39.3\%: pensions 14.3,
  health 5.4, unemployment benefits 0.6 and the consumption floor 0.1,
  government consumption 7.0, defence 1.7, investment 4.2, education 2.6,
  the lump-sum transfer 3.5. The pension share sits 0.3 points and the
  health share 0.1 above the calibration's because output in the transition's first year is 1.8\% below the
  base-year cross-section's, households working less ahead of the higher
  wage of 2026.
\item Two revenue lines need a word on what they stand for in the accounts.
  The capital income tax applies one rate to the return on all household
  wealth, set so that its revenue in 2023 equals the taxes on capital
  income in the accounts, 3.7\% of GDP: the taxes on corporate income, the
  taxes on interest and the part of the personal income tax that falls on
  capital income. Its rise with the wealth-output ratio assumes the wealth
  of the old is taxed at that rate as it accumulates. The bequest tax is the
  confiscation of accidental bequests: Greece's inheritance and gift taxes
  yield 0.1\% of GDP, but the model has no property taxation, and recurrent
  property taxes in the accounts (about 2.0\% of GDP) are of the size of
  the model's bequest line in 2023. The line stands for them in level; its
  rise with the age structure has no counterpart in a property tax.

  to 2027 and potential growth after. The model has no cycle.

\section*{Policy experiments}\vspace{-0.2cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
Design:
\begin{itemize}
\item Two permanent spending increases start in 2023, each worth 2\% of
  output. Neither enters utility.
\item Government consumption $G$ rises by 2\% of each year's output.
\item Public investment $I_g$ rises by 2\% of 2023 output and stays there in
  detrended per-capita terms, because public capital enters production and
  an increase set as a share of output would depend on its own effect.
\item Debt financing: the primary deficit is borrowed abroad at $r_B$ and no
  tax changes.
\item Labour-tax financing: $\tau_l$ rises by a constant $\Delta\tau_l$ from
  2023, set so that at the start of 2202, the last year of the horizon,
  either the debt ratio or net foreign assets over output are back at their
  baseline values.
\item The output-tax path, the lump-sum transfer per adult, the unemployment
  path and the stock-flow adjustment stay at the baseline's; the EU transfer
  stays at the baseline's share of each run's own output; education is
  priced at each run's own wage from the base-year level.
\item The policy is announced in 2023. The wealth each cohort holds that year
  is the same as in the baseline.
\item Net foreign assets are household foreign assets less government debt.
\item The cumulative multiplier is the sum of the output response over the
  sum of the spending increase, both as aggregate flows over the horizon.
\item In Table~\ref{tab:fiscal}, debt is at the end of the year over the
  year's output. The experiments solve their own baseline under the same
  rule; its rate from 2026 is 4.41\% against the report baseline's 4.35,
  because it prices education on the base-year output of the cross-section
  while the report baseline prices it on the transition's own 2023 output,
  1.8\% lower, a difference of 0.05 points of output in spending. Its debt
  ratio is 86\%
  in 2070, 53\% in 2080, $-40$\% in 2100 and $-649$\% in 2200, the rate
  staying at its 2026 value while the balance rises; its net foreign assets
  are 8.2 times output in 2200. Without a terminal adjustment the economy is
  not at rest at the end of the horizon: the government's net asset position
  is still growing, and the evaluator flags the change in the debt ratio
  over 2070--80 ($-32$ points) and the terminal drift of debt, net foreign
  assets and pension assets (1--2\%). The 2023--32 column is the mean
  deviation over those years.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Government consumption (Figure~\ref{fig:G}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Debt-financed, it leaves output, consumption and hours exactly where
  they were. It enters neither utility nor production, and the debt is held
  abroad, so no household budget changes. The multiplier is zero.
\item The debt ratio climbs to 190\% of output in 2060 and is $-132$\% in
  2200, 520 points above the baseline's.
\item Bringing the ratio back to the baseline's by 2202 takes
  $\Delta\tau_l=4.31$ percentage points. Hours and output fall 0.6\% in 2023
  and 0.4\% on average over 2023--32, and are within 0.1\% of the baseline
  from 2050. Consumption is 3.6\% below the baseline in 2023 and 4.7\%
  below in 2100.
\item Returning net foreign assets to their baseline ratio instead takes 4.53
  points and leaves the debt ratio 26 points below the baseline's in 2200.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Public investment (Figure~\ref{fig:Ig}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Public capital per person rises from 0.70 of base-year output to 0.98
  in 2050 and 1.03 in 2100.
\item Debt-financed, output is 0.9\% below the baseline in 2023, because
  households expect higher wages and work less now. It is 0.4\% above the
  baseline on average over 2023--32, 2.7\% in 2050 and 2.8\% in 2100.
  Consumption is 0.9\% above the baseline in 2023 and 2.9\% above in 2100.
\item The cumulative multiplier is 0.21 over 2023--32 and 1.42 over the
  horizon.
\item The debt ratio reaches 180\% in 2060 and is $-229$\% in 2200, 420
  points above the baseline's.
\item The labour-tax rules need 3.51 points (debt target) and 3.69 points
  (net-foreign-asset target). Output is 1.3\% below the baseline in 2023
  under both and 2.6\% above in 2050. Consumption ends 1.1\% and 1.3\% below
  in 2100.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Temporary cut in public health spending (design):
\begin{itemize}
\item The experiment reproduces the fall in public health spending during
  the adjustment programme. From 2010 to 2014 government health spending
  fell from 6.6\% to 4.6\% of GDP, while health spending not paid by the
  government rose from 3.0\% to 3.4\% of GDP and fell by 11\% in euros
  (Eurostat \texttt{hlth\_sha11\_hf}: government and compulsory schemes,
  and current health expenditure less those schemes). Total health
  spending fell by a third in euros.
\item In the model the government pays a share $\kappa$ of each household's
  medical spending $m$ and the household pays the rest. A fall in
  government spending with household spending nearly unchanged as a share
  of output therefore requires both a lower coverage rate $\kappa$ and a
  lower level of medical spending $m$. A cut in $\kappa$ alone would shift
  the whole reduction onto households.
\item $\kappa$ and $m$ are set so that, on the baseline's output, government
  health spending falls by 1.29 points of output and household health
  spending rises by 0.26 points, the mean changes over 2011--15 relative
  to 2010. Both are held from 2026 to 2030 and return to their baseline
  values in 2031. The five-year mean matches the cumulative cut in the
  data; the 2014 change held for five years would overstate it by about
  60\%.
\item Household health spending is measured as current health expenditure
  less government and compulsory schemes, the same concept as the
  household share in the calibration (total health spending less public
  health spending). Out-of-pocket payments alone rose by 0.17 points over
  the same years.
\item Medical spending enters neither utility nor survival, so the lower
  $m$ is a saving to households and government with no cost in health
  outcomes. Two further runs, one cutting $\kappa$ alone and one cutting $m$
  alone, separate the effect of the lower level of spending from the shift
  of costs between government and households.
\item The cut is not anticipated: households learn of it in 2026, with the
  wealth they hold under the baseline. It is financed by debt, by a
  permanent change in $\tau_l$ from 2026 that returns the debt ratio to the
  baseline's by 2202, or by a change in $\tau_l$ over 2026--30 only.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Appendix: reference sizes for the public investment increase}\vspace{-0.2cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
The experiments raise public investment by 2\% of output for good, from
4.2\% in 2023. The sizes discussed in the EU since 2024:
\begin{itemize}
\item Draghi report (September 2024): \EUR{750}--800bn a year of additional
  investment in the EU, 4.4--4.7\% of 2023 GDP, public and private
  together. Readings of its public share range from a fifth, the historical
  share of public money in EU investment, to a half, that is 1 to 2.3\% of
  GDP a year.
\item The Commission's estimate of the investment the 2030 climate target
  needs on top of current spending: \EUR{477}bn a year, 3\% of 2022 GDP, of
  which the public sector would cover 20--25\%, so 0.6--0.75\% of GDP.
\item Germany's infrastructure and climate fund (2025): \EUR{500}bn over
  twelve years, about \EUR{42}bn a year, close to 1\% of GDP, financed by
  borrowing.
\item The EU's defence escape clause (2025--26; Greece is among the states
  using it): up to 1.5\% of GDP a year above the planned path for four
  years. Defence is government consumption in the model's accounts, not
  investment.
\item Greece's recovery and resilience plan: \EUR{36}bn over 2021--26, 17\%
  of 2022 GDP, of which \EUR{18.2}bn in grants; about 2.9\% of GDP a year,
  grants 1.5\%. The OECD Economic Outlook of December 2025 puts the public
  spending it finances at 2.1\% of GDP in 2025 and 4\% in 2026, its peak
  year.
\end{itemize}
A lasting 2\% of output sits at the top of this range: it is close to the
half-public reading of the Draghi report and the recovery plan's peak year,
while the German fund and the public part of the climate gap are about 1\%
of GDP, and every instance above is temporary, grant-financed or
ring-fenced rather than a permanent debt-financed increase. A central case
of 1\% of output with 2\% as the upper case would rest on this record; the
recovery plan's profile, 2.7\% of GDP a year for six years and nothing
after, is the Greek counterpart of a temporary increase.
