\vspace{-0.6cm}

\noindent\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Small open economy with overlapping generations, solved in detrended
  per-capita units. A hat denotes $\hat x_t=X_t/(Z_tN_t)$, with
  $Z_t=(1+g)^t$ labour productivity and $N_t$ the population. Stocks
  accumulate at the gross rate $\Gamma_t=(1+g)(1+n_t)$.
\item On the balanced growth path $\hat x$ is constant, levels grow at
  $\Gamma-1$ and per-capita quantities at $g$.
\item Preferences are $\log c-\nu\ell^{1+\varphi}/(1+\varphi)$. The period is
  a year.
\item Households hold domestic capital and foreign assets at the world return
  $r$. Government debt is held by official creditors at the real rate
  $r_{B,t}$: the data's real effective rate to 2025, the Commission's
  projection over 2026--60 and 2\% from 2070.
\item Firms pay a tax $\tau_y$ on gross output, so that
  $(1-\tau_y)\alpha Y/K=r+\delta$ and $w=(1-\tau_y)(1-\alpha)Y/L$. It stands
  for the taxes on production and the other revenue the model has no line
  for, net of the spending it has no line for. The rate is set in 2023 to the
  outturn primary balance and held to 2060.
\item Every adult receives a lump-sum transfer, the same at every age and
  education level and untaxed, worth $\lambda$ of output per person, output
  taken as a centred five-year average: the cash and in-kind benefits
  outside pensions, unemployment and health. Households choose next
  period's wealth on the asset grid, so a transfer tied to each year's own
  output would let their choices and the transfer sustain a two-year
  alternation in hours.
\item The government buys education, with the number of pupils from
  EUROPOP2023 and the cost per pupil moving with the wage, and receives the
  net transfer from the EU budget.
\item TFP $A$ is set so that $\hat y=1$ in 2023, so ratios to output read as
  levels.
\item Wealth sits on a grid of 100 nodes from 0 to 50 times output per person,
  denser near zero (spacing $u^{1.5}$). Saving choices are grid nodes.
  Aggregates come from the distribution over household states carried forward
  by age, not from simulation.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\footnotesize
Sources for the parameters not taken straight from a published table:
\begin{itemize}
\item $K_g/Y$: the IMF ICSD public capital stock for 2019, carried to 2023 by
  perpetual inventory with national-accounts public investment and real GDP.
\item $\delta_g$: $I_g/K_g-(\Gamma_0-1)$ at the 2023 investment share, which
  keeps public capital per person constant; the perpetual-inventory estimate
  from the same stock over 2000--2019 is 0.002 lower.
\item $r_B$: a real rate by year. For 2023 and 2024 the data's real effective
  rate, interest payments over the previous year's debt deflated by GDP
  deflator growth ($-4.1$ and $-1.1$\%); for 2025 the projection's 2026
  nominal rate deflated by 2025 deflator growth ($-1.0$\%); over 2026--60
  the real effective rate of the Commission's debt projection for Greece
  (Spring 2026 forecast, Debt Sustainability Monitor layout): $-1.1$\% in
  2026, $-0.6$\% in 2030, 1.7\% in 2040, 1.6\% in 2050 and 1.2\% in 2060;
  linear to 2\% over 2061--70, the Monitor's long-run real rate on market
  debt; 2\% after.
\item $\tau_y$: the rate that gives the outturn primary balance of 2023
  (1.95\% of GDP) at the calibrated household block, solved together with
  $A$. From 2061 it moves linearly over ten years to the rate at which the
  debt ratio in 2080 equals its 2070 value, and stays there.
\item $G/Y$, $D/Y$, $I_g/Y$, $e_0$, $\lambda$ and the pension and health
  targets: general government accounts 2023 (Eurostat \texttt{gov\_10a\_main},
  \texttt{gov\_10a\_exp}), purchases net of the imputed contributions of
  civil servants. Own wage bill and intermediate consumption outside
  defence, health and education 7.0\% of GDP; defence 1.66\%; gross fixed
  capital formation 4.0\%; education 2.6\%; cash benefits other than
  pensions and unemployment benefits plus transfers in kind outside health
  3.5\%; pensions 14.0\% (old age, survivors and disability, the Ageing
  Report's concept); public health 5.3\% of a total of 8.4\%; actual social
  contributions 11.2\%.
\item Health cost by age: the Ageing Report's per-capita public health
  spending by five-year age group for the EU14 (2024 Ageing Report, Graph
  I.2.2), each group at its midpoint from 27 to 97, linear between midpoints,
  flat outside, normalised to a population-weighted mean of one over the 2023
  cross-section. The level is $m$, set in the SMM.
\item Net transfer from the EU budget: current and capital transfers
  received from EU institutions less own resources paid, 1.9\% of GDP in
  2023, 1.8\% in 2024, 2.7\% in 2025 and 2026 (Recovery and Resilience
  Facility grants), 1.0\% from 2027, the level before the Facility.
\item $b_{min}$: the national pension, \EUR{413.76} a month from January
  2023, over output per person aged 25--99 (\EUR{224.7}bn of GDP over 7.92
  million people, \EUR{28{,}353} each). The amount is from the legislation
  and the 2024 Ageing Report country fiche; Eurostat reports only aggregate
  pension spending.
\item Retirement age: the average effective age at which people start an
  old-age, early or disability pension, 63.8 in 2022 against a statutory 67.
  To 2070 it follows the Ageing Report's projection (country fiche, Table 4):
  65.5 in 2030, 66.4 in 2040, 66.6 in 2050, 67.4 in 2060 and 67.9 in 2070,
  linear in between. After 2070 it rises by 0.75 of the change in life
  expectancy at 65 (men and women averaged, EUROPOP2023), the ratio of the
  fiche's rise in the effective age (4.1 years) to its rise in the statutory
  age (5.5 years) over 2022--70, and reaches 69.7 in 2100. A cohort retires
  at the age in force when it gets there: 67.7 for the cohort entering in
  2023, 69.4 for the one entering in 2050. The model is annual, so each cohort is split between the two whole
  ages around its average, in the proportions that reproduce it.
\item Pension index: the replacement rate in year $t$ is $\rho^{pens}$ times
  an index equal to one in 2023. The index is the Ageing Report's average
  pension relative to GDP per employed person, pension spending over GDP
  divided by pensioners over employment (country fiche, Tables 6 and 10):
  0.85 in 2030, 0.77 in 2040, 0.67 in 2050 and 0.62 from 2060, linear in
  between. It applies to every pension in payment, $b_{min}$ included.
  Households know the path from entry, as they know their cohort's retirement
  age and survival rates.
\item Unemployment rates by education: 2023, ages 25--64 (Eurostat
  \texttt{lfsa\_urgaed}). Each rate follows an index by calendar year: the
  2024 and 2025 outturns for ages 25--64 (9.5 and 8.3\% against 10.2\% in
  2023), the Spring 2026 forecast for 2026--27 (8.3 and 7.9\% for ages
  15--74, scaled by the 2023 ratio of the two age groups), a linear path to
  the 2024 Ageing Report's 2050 rate (6.6\% for ages 20--64, scaled to
  5.6\%) and 5.5\% from 2055. The separation rate of each year gives every
  cohort its own income process by age, known from entry.
\item Education shares: the population aged 25--99 in 2023, from the labour
  force survey for ages 25--74 (\texttt{lfsa\_pgaed}) and the 2021 census for
  ages 75--99 (\texttt{cens\_21ae\_r2}).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Demographics}
\vspace{-0.1cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
\item The calibration starts from the measured 2023 age distribution, not a
  stationary population. A stationary population with the same dependency
  ratio would miss the large cohorts about to retire, which drive the
  projected rise in the ratio.
\item Each cohort faces the survival rates of its own birth year: the
  historical life table to 2023 (Eurostat \texttt{demo\_mlifetable}) and the
  EUROPOP2023 mortality assumption from 2024. The historical table ends in an
  open group at 85, so ages 85--99 before 2024 carry the 2022 assumption;
  those entries concern ages a cohort has already passed and enter no
  decision.
\item From 2023, survival and the size of entering cohorts follow the
  EUROPOP2023 baseline to 2100, in the baseline and in every experiment.
  After 2100 mortality stays at its 2100 level and the growth of entering
  cohorts goes to zero by 2120. The age distribution is constant from 2194 on.
\item The size of each entering cohort, measured for the cohorts alive in
  2023 and projected to 2100, is smoothed with a centred five-year moving
  average in logs. Adjacent single-year cohorts in the Greek age pyramid
  differ by 5\% on average and by up to 24\% at the oldest ages, and every
  budget line priced by cohort would otherwise jump with whichever cohort
  enters, retires or dies in a given year.
\item Matched: death rates by year and age, exactly; the population aged
  25--99, within 0.3\% in every year to 2100; the 2023 age distribution, up
  to the smoothing.
\item Missed: the age composition after 2023, because net migration at ages
  26--84 is replaced by a change in the cohort entering at 25, so the model
  has too many prime-age and old people until the 2050s and too few after
  (persons aged 65--99 per person aged 25--64: 0.88 in 2050 and 0.70 in 2080,
  against 0.82 and 0.72 in the projection); people under 25 and over 99 (the
  100+ group is 0.04\% of the population in 2023 and 0.5\% in 2100); what has
  happened since 2022 (recorded births in 2023--24 are 11--14\% below the
  projection's).
\item Tables~\ref{tab:demography_short} and \ref{tab:demography} set the
  projection against the data since 2022. On 1 January 2025 the population was 10.37 million, 0.5\% above
  the projection, because net migration was 30 thousand in 2023 and 54
  thousand in 2024 against the projection's 5 and $-2$; live births in
  2023--24 were 10--12\% below the projection's; life expectancy at 65 in
  2024 was 18.7 years for men and 21.8 for women, 0.5 and 0.3 below the
  assumption. The old-age ratio on 1 January 2025, 40.9 per 100, is the
  projection's.
\item Net migration at every age is put into the cohort entering at 25
  (Figure~\ref{fig:demography}, top left). It is negative until the 2050s,
  hence the fall in 2024 and the movements through the 2030s.
\item The drop in 2048 is EUROPOP2023's own: its 2022 birth cohort is 9\%
  smaller than 2021's, matching the recorded fall in live births from 85.3 to
  75.9 thousand (Eurostat \texttt{demo\_fasec}). The projection starts from
  the population of 1 January 2022, so later cohorts are projected: 79.2 and
  77.8 thousand births for 2023 and 2024 against 71.2 and 68.3 recorded, so
  the cohorts entering from 2049 on are 10--14\% larger than recorded births
  imply.
\item The share aged 65--99 rises from 0.30 to 0.47 by 2050 as the cohorts
  born in the 1960s and 1970s retire. Persons aged 65--99 per person aged
  25--64 peak at 0.91 in 2056, from 0.43 in 2023. The retired per non-retired
  person rise from 0.46 to 0.80 in 2055 and fall back to 0.49 by 2100,
  because the retirement age rises.
\item Because the 2023 population is not stationary, the economy is in
  transition even with no policy change. Prices do not move: the return is
  set abroad and the wage follows from it. Ageing reaches households through
  the retirement age, higher for later cohorts, and through the pension index,
  which lowers the pension a given career earns.
\item Output per person falls to 2051 (Figure~\ref{fig:aggregates}) and
  pensions rise to 2050 (Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal}) as the share aged 40--64
  shrinks and the retired per non-retired person grow. Both turn around once
  the smaller cohorts reach old age and the retirement age keeps rising.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Baseline transition}\vspace{-0.2cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
Aggregates (Figure~\ref{fig:aggregates}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Domestic capital and hours move with output while the output tax is
  constant: the world return fixes $K^{dom}/L$ and public capital per person
  is constant. The tax's fall after 2060 raises the capital-output ratio by
  6\% and the wage by 8\%.
\item Private investment is $I_t=\Gamma_t K^{dom}_{t+1}-(1-\delta)K^{dom}_t$,
  plotted as a 5-year centred moving average.
\item Household wealth is $A=K^{dom}+NFA+B$; the figure's $NFA$ is household
  foreign assets, $A-K^{dom}$.
\item Output per person rises 1.4\% between 2025 and 2030 as unemployment
  falls from 8.3 to 7.0\%, falls with the age structure to 0.83 of its 2023
  level in 2060, and recovers to 0.97 by 2070 and 1.05 by 2100 on the rising
  retirement age and the lower output tax.
\item Real growth averages 0.60\% a year over 2026--60: 1.6\% in 2026--30,
  0.5\% in 2031--40, 0.1\% in 2041--50 and 0.7\% in 2051--60, against 1.0,
  0.9, 1.2 and 1.2\% in the Commission's projection. The yearly path moves
  by up to half a percentage point around its trend, because households
  choose next period's wealth on a grid; the figure shows a 5-year centred
  mean.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Government budget (Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal}, Table~\ref{tab:projection}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Tax rates, $G/Y$, defence$/Y$ and $I_g$ per person are fixed. Pensions
  follow the index and the number of retirees. Health spending and the tax
  bases follow the age structure; unemployment benefits fall with the
  unemployment rate. Education follows the school-age population and the
  wage. The lump-sum transfer is $\lambda$ of output per person, output
  taken as a centred five-year average.
\item The output tax at 5.95\% of output to 2060 carries the revenue and the
  spending the model does not describe. The primary balance is the model's
  own; the Commission's projection is a comparison.
\item Debt is the end-of-year stock over the year's output,
  $d_t=d_{t-1}(1+r_{B,t})/(1+g_t)-pb_t+sfa_t$ from 1.64 in 2023, with
  $g_t=\Gamma_{t-1}\hat y_t/\hat y_{t-1}-1$. The years 2024 and 2025 are
  history: their ratios are the data's (154.2\%) and the projection's
  starting value (146.1\%), and the flow that reconciles each with the
  recursion is recorded as that year's stock-flow adjustment, $-4.9$ and
  $-0.8$\% of output. The model has neither the real growth of those years
  (2.1\% a year) nor their real rates ($-1.1$ and about $-1.0$\%). Over
  2026--60 the adjustment is the projection's (0.8--1.1\% of output a year
  to 2032, interest on the official loans whose payment is deferred). After
  2060 it is zero.
\item The primary balance is 2.0\% of output in 2023 by construction, 3.4\%
  in 2025 and between 2.2 and 4.0\% in every year to 2060. Debt falls to
  121\% in 2030, 94\% in 2040, 80\% in 2050 and 63\% in 2060; the projection
  has 124, 111, 111 and 103\%.
\item From 2061 the tax rate moves linearly to 0.82\% in 2070, the rate
  at which the debt ratio in 2080 equals its 2070 value, 53\%, and stays
  there. The balance is $-0.7$\% of output in 2070 and 2.2\% in 2100; the
  ratio is 23\% in 2100 and below zero from 2110.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Why the debt paths differ:
\begin{itemize}
\item The balance. Replacing the model's primary balance over 2026--60 with
  the Commission's, everything else as in the model, puts debt at 124\% in
  2060 instead of 63\%. The Commission holds the structural balance before
  the cost of ageing at 2.3\% of GDP, with a constant revenue ratio, and adds
  the Ageing Report's spending paths. The model's revenue rises from 40.8 to
  45.0\% of output by 2050 (consumption tax +1.8 points, capital income tax
  +1.5, bequest tax +1.7) while its spending rises from 38.8 to 42.6\%
  (pensions +1.0, health +2.6), so its balance holds near 2.5\% where the
  Commission's falls to 0.4\%.
\item Growth. Replacing the model's output growth with the projection's puts
  debt at 49\% in 2060 instead of 63\%: 0.60\% a year against 1.08\%. The
  productivity assumption is the Ageing Report's in both, and the model's
  unemployment rate follows the Report's long-run path. The difference is the
  participation of 55--74-year-olds, which the Report raises beyond what the
  retirement age delivers, and hours per person, which fall with the age
  structure.
\item The rate. The model uses the projection's real effective rate year by
  year over 2026--60, so the rate contributes nothing to the gap in that
  period.
\item With the Commission's balance, growth and rate the recursion
  reproduces the projection's 103.4\% in 2060.
\item Unemployment. Holding the rate at its 2023 level, 10.2\%, in every
  year, with the same parameters and the same rules, lowers the balance by
  0.3\% of output a year (benefits 0.2--0.3 points higher, output 2--3\%
  lower) and raises the 2060 ratio by 17 points; the terminal output tax is
  then 1.3\% instead of 0.8\%.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Spending, revenue and the balance (Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal}, Table~\ref{tab:projection}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Pensions fall from 14.0\% of output in 2023 to 12.2\% in 2030, because
  the index falls faster than the number of retirees grows, climb to 15.0\%
  in 2050 as the large cohorts retire, and fall to 14.3\% in 2060 and 11.4\%
  in 2070. The Ageing Report has 14.5\% of GDP in 2022, 12.7\% in 2030,
  14.0\% in 2050, 12.7\% in 2060 and 12.0\% in 2070. The model is 1.0 point
  above the Report in 2050 and 1.6 above in 2060, and 0.6 below in 2070,
  when its retirement age keeps rising.
\item Public health spending rises from 5.3\% of output to 7.9\% by 2050
  and 8.4\% by 2060, with the unit cost by age growing at the trend; the Ageing Report has
  +0.6 points by 2040, with unit costs growing with GDP per capita.
\item Education moves between 2.4 and 2.8\% of output with the school-age
  population; unemployment benefits fall from 0.6 to 0.3\%.
\item Revenue is 40.8\% of output in 2023: consumption tax 8.9, labour
  income tax 6.6, contributions 11.2, capital income tax 3.6, bequest tax
  2.7, output tax 6.0, EU transfer 1.9. Spending is 38.8\%: pensions 14.0,
  health 5.3, unemployment benefits and the floor 0.6, government
  consumption 7.0, defence 1.7, investment 4.1, education 2.6, the lump-sum
  transfer 3.5.
\item The projection's growth (Figure~\ref{fig:projection}) is the forecast
  to 2027 and potential growth after. The model has no cycle.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Policy experiments}\vspace{-0.2cm}

\noindent\footnotesize
Design:
\begin{itemize}
\item Two permanent spending increases start in 2023, each worth 2\% of
  output. Neither enters utility.
\item Government consumption $G$ rises by 2\% of each year's output.
\item Public investment $I_g$ rises by 2\% of 2023 output and stays there in
  detrended per-capita terms, because public capital enters production and
  an increase set as a share of output would depend on its own effect.
\item Debt financing: the primary deficit is borrowed abroad at $r_B$ and no
  tax changes.
\item Labour-tax financing: $\tau_l$ rises by a constant $\Delta\tau_l$ from
  2023, set so that in 2202, the last year of the horizon, either the debt
  ratio or net foreign assets over output are back at their baseline values.
\item The output-tax path, the lump-sum transfer per adult, the transfer from
  the EU budget and the stock-flow adjustment stay at the baseline's;
  education is priced at each run's own wage.
\item The policy is announced in 2023. The wealth each cohort holds that year
  is the same as in the baseline.
\item Net foreign assets are household foreign assets less government debt.
\item The cumulative multiplier is the sum of the output response over the
  sum of the spending increase, both as aggregate flows over the horizon.
\item In Table~\ref{tab:fiscal}, debt is at the end of the year over the
  year's output. The baseline's ratio is 52\% over 2070--80, 24\% in 2100 and
  $-226$\% in 2200 (the output tax stays at its 2070 rate while the balance
  turns positive again); its net foreign assets are 4.2 times output in
  2200. The 2023--32 column is the mean deviation over those years.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Government consumption (Figure~\ref{fig:G}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Debt-financed, it leaves output, consumption and hours exactly where
  they were. It enters neither utility nor production, and the debt is held
  abroad, so no household budget changes. The multiplier is zero.
\item The debt ratio climbs to 150\% of output in 2060 and 279\% in 2200.
\item Bringing the ratio back to the baseline's by 2202 takes
  $\Delta\tau_l=4.22$ percentage points. Hours and output fall 1.5\% in 2023
  and 0.5\% on average over 2023--32, and are back at the baseline by 2050.
  Consumption is 2.6\% below the baseline in 2023 and 4.6\% below in 2100.
\item Returning net foreign assets to their baseline ratio instead takes 4.44
  points and leaves the debt ratio 27 points below the baseline's in 2200.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Public investment (Figure~\ref{fig:Ig}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Public capital per person rises from 0.70 of 2023 output to 0.98 in
  2050 and 1.04 in 2100.
\item Debt-financed, output is 2.2\% below the baseline in 2023, because
  households expect higher wages and work less now. It is 0.3\% above the
  baseline on average over 2023--32, 2.6\% in 2050 and 2.9\% in 2100.
  Consumption is 2.2\% above the baseline in 2023 and 2.9\% above in 2100.
\item The cumulative multiplier is 0.17 over 2023--32 and 1.45 over the
  horizon.
\item The debt ratio reaches 145\% in 2060 and 188\% in 2200.
\item The labour-tax rules need 3.49 points (debt target) and 3.68 points
  (net-foreign-asset target). Output is 2.5\% below the baseline in 2023
  under both and 2.7\% above in 2050. Consumption ends 1.1\% and 1.3\% below
  in 2100.
\end{itemize}
